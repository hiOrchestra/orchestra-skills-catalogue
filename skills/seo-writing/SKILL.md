# SEO Writing — a piece the searcher finds, reads and finishes

Search engines reward what readers reward: the page that actually answers the
question the person typed, quickly, and keeps them there. This skill is that
discipline — intent first, then structure, then the small technical pieces
(title, meta, headings, links) — applied to a piece you or the person is writing.

## Related skills
- **write-article** — drafts the piece; this skill sets the search brief it
  writes to and checks the result.
- **content-plan** — decides which pieces to write; this skill shapes one of them.
- **fact-check** — verifies the claims; search visibility does not excuse a
  wrong number.
(If a referenced skill is not installed, do the equivalent inline.)

## When to use
- A piece whose readers arrive from search, or an existing page that should.
- **Do NOT use** for a newsletter, a personal essay, a post for people who
  already follow them — writing to a keyword would make those worse. Do not use
  for site-level work (speed, crawling, redirects): say it is out of scope.

## Workflow
1. **Name the query and the intent.** What does the person type, and what do
   they want when they type it — to learn, to compare, to buy, to do a task?
   Use `web_search` to read the top results for the query: what they cover,
   what they miss, what format wins (guide, list, comparison, tool).
2. **Decide the angle that earns the click and the stay.** What this piece
   will answer better, sooner or more honestly than what ranks. If nothing —
   say so; a piece with no angle will not rank and should not be written.
3. **Write the search brief**: primary query, 2–4 related queries the same
   piece answers, the intent, the angle, the format, the length the top results
   suggest, the questions the piece must answer (from "People also ask" and the
   gaps you found). Hand it to `write-article`, or write to it.
4. **Structure for both readers.** The answer in the first 100 words. H2s that
   are the sub-questions, in the order a person would ask them. Short
   paragraphs. A table or a list where the top results make people scroll for
   the same information.
5. **The small pieces.** Title tag ≤ 60 characters, the query near the front,
   written for a human. Meta description ≤ 155 characters, a reason to click,
   not a summary. One H1. Descriptive image alt text. 2–4 internal links to
   the site's own related pages (ask for the URLs or list the site), and
   outbound links to the primary sources the piece cites.
6. **Check before delivery** against the Standards, then deliver the piece plus
   the metadata block under Output.

## Standards
- The query appears where it is natural — title, first paragraph, one heading —
  and nowhere it is not. If a sentence exists only to hold a keyword, cut it.
- The piece answers the question before it sells anything.
- Every claim that could be checked is checked; a sourced number beats an
  adjective.
- Nothing is copied or lightly rewritten from a ranking page. Read to find
  the gap, then write your own.
- Never promise a ranking or a traffic number. Say what the piece does well
  and what is outside its control.

## Output
The piece, plus a metadata block:
- **Primary query / related queries / intent**
- **Title tag** (with character count) · **Meta description** (with count) ·
  **URL slug**
- **Headings outline** (H1, H2s)
- **Internal links** (anchor → URL) · **Sources linked**
- **What the ranking pages miss that this covers** — 2–3 lines.

## Defaults
- No keyword given → derive the query from the topic and the audience, state
  it, and proceed.
- Length: what the top results and the intent suggest, not a fixed number;
  say why.
- Existing page → keep the URL, improve the piece, list what changed and why.
