# The question bank

Take what applies. Every question is written the way you would ask it; the
note under it says why it matters and what the answer decides. Group the ones
you pick into one message, ordered from the biggest decision to the smallest.

Questions whose answer is already on the instance — files they sent, a live
site, `USER.md`, an earlier conversation — are not asked. Say what you found
instead: "I see you already have the domain — I'll use that."

---

## Always — whatever is being built

**Who is this for, and what should they be able to do when they arrive?**
The audience picks the aesthetic, the vocabulary and the front page. "For my
readers" and "for the two people on my team" are different builds.

**What does "done" look like for the first version?**
Name the first concrete thing they will judge. It sets the scope of the first
delivery and stops a build from sprawling past what they wanted to see.

**Is there anything that already exists — a site, a logo, colours, a
document, a spreadsheet you work from?**
Existing material is the brief's raw input and the strongest signal of what
they will recognise as theirs. For a redesign it is not optional input.

**Where do you want to hear from me about this — here, Telegram, email?**
Decides where reports, drafts and questions go. Do not assume the channel you
are on is the one they check.

## Publication — a blog, a magazine, a column, a newsletter archive

**What is a post, for you? When you sit down to write one, what does it have?**
This answer *is* the content model. A woodworker's piece has a material and
dimensions and whether it was commissioned; a columnist's has a section; a
researcher's has citations. Model that, not "title, body, date".

**How do you want the front page to work — newest first, by section, as a
gallery, something else?**
Reverse-chronological is a default, not an answer. Someone with forty pieces
needs a way in that is not scrolling.

**How will you get material to me — a document and a photo? A folder? A
spreadsheet with dates?**
Decides the intake procedure and what "eligible to publish" means. One
publisher sends a Word file, a PNG and a calendar with a status column, and
nothing goes out unless all three agree — that rule came from asking this.

**When should things go live — as soon as you send them, on a date you set,
at a fixed hour? And who presses publish, you or me?**
Publishing is the owner's decision unless they hand it over explicitly.
Scheduling by date is cheap to build and changes the workflow entirely.

**Can readers respond — comments, reactions, a contact form? Should comments
appear immediately or wait for you?**
Each is a public write endpoint with its own abuse surface. Comments published
immediately keep a small site alive; a queue nobody clears kills it. Let them
choose knowing that.

**Do you have a domain? Do you want people to be able to subscribe — by feed
or by email?**
Domain: theirs to own and point. Subscribing: a feed costs nothing; email
costs a service and a decision about who holds the addresses.

**How should it feel?** — offer two or three named directions from the
aesthetics catalogue that fit the subject and audience, and ask which is
closer. If they say "you decide", derive it from the subject and say so.

**What must never happen?**
"Never publish before I say so", "never delete a piece to make room", "never
change the front page without telling me". These become the operating
skill's protections.

## Presence — a landing page, a portfolio, a company site

**What is the one thing a visitor should do — write to you, book, buy,
download, just understand?**
One call to action, stated by them. A page with three equal buttons is a page
that did not know.

**Who arrives, and from where?** An ad, a search, a link in a bio, a card at a
fair. Decides what the first screen must say.

**What proof do you have — clients, numbers, work, press, reviews?**
Real material beats copy. Ask for it before writing a word of "trusted by".

**What copy exists, and who writes what is missing — you or me?**
If you write it, it is a draft they approve; say so.

**Do you already have a brand — logo, colours, type, photography? A site you
like the feel of? One you dislike?**
Directions and anti-directions. Then offer two or three from the catalogue.

**Domain and hosting: do you have them, and is anything already running
there — including email on that domain?**
Moving a domain moves all of it. Mail stops if the records are not carried.

**What "done" looks like** — usually: the page is live on their domain, the
form reaches them, and they have approved the copy.

## Tool — an app, a dashboard, an internal thing for a team

**Who uses it and what do they need to do — list the three or four actions.**
The actions are the screens. Everything else is decoration.

**What is the thing being managed — a client, an order, a booking, a request —
and what does one contain? What is required?**
The content model again. Ask what they write on paper today.

**Who can do what? Is there anything only you should be able to do?**
Roles, or the absence of them. Decides whether there is a login at all.

**Where does the data come from and where does it need to go — a
spreadsheet, another system, an export?**
Import once, sync forever, or neither — three different builds.

**What would make you stop using it in a week?** Their fear names the first
thing to get right.

**Access: a public link, a password, per-person logins?**

## A change to something that exists

**What is wrong with it today — what happens that should not, or does not
happen that should?**
Change requests are usually symptoms. Get the story.

**What must stay exactly as it is?**
The most important question for a redesign. Preserve versus overhaul is their
call, and the wrong guess undoes work they liked.

**Who is affected while it changes — readers, customers, a team using it
today?** Decides whether the change goes live behind a preview first.

**Is there a deadline or an event this is for?**

## Asking about the look — a note

Do not ask "what colours do you like?" Ask what the thing is for and who it is
for, then propose: "From what you have told me this could go one of two ways:
*quiet and editorial* — generous type, lots of white, the writing carries it;
or *warm and crafted* — texture, photography first, a hand-made feel. Which
is closer?" The names come from the `design-taste-frontend` aesthetics
catalogue; a person can react to a direction, not to a hex code.

If they give a reference site, read it for its *language* (density, type,
motion, tone) — not to copy it.
